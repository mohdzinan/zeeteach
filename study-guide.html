IE 1 · 3 MARKS · 11 PERIODS

Indian Economy on the Eve of Independence

Colonial economic policy reshaped production and trade around the needs of the British economy. At independence, India inherited a largely agrarian economy with low productivity, a narrow modern industrial base, and infrastructure built mainly to serve colonial administration and commerce.

How the colonial pattern worked

India supplied primary products such as cotton, jute, indigo, and raw silk, while importing finished manufactured goods. The decline of traditional handicrafts weakened local producers. Agriculture employed most people, but zamindari and other land-revenue arrangements, limited irrigation, low investment, and old technology held productivity back.

Railways, ports, communications, and power expanded, but their routes and priorities reflected colonial trade and administration. National-income estimates were limited; Indian economists such as Dadabhai Naoroji and V. K. R. V. Rao attempted to describe economic conditions.

Remember

Explain the colonial legacy through connected evidence: agriculture, industry, foreign trade, and infrastructure. Avoid describing the economy as simply “unchanged”; colonial rule altered what India produced and who benefited from trade.

Answer focus Use four short headings: agriculture, industry, trade, infrastructure. Add one specific detail under each.

IE 2 · 4 MARKS · 11 PERIODS

Indian Economy, 1950–1990

After independence, India used economic planning to build productive capacity and address poverty and inequality. The policy approach combined a large public sector with private activity and used protection to support domestic industry.

Four goals of planning

Growth means increasing the economy’s capacity to produce. Modernisation means adopting new technology and changing social outlooks. Self-reliance means reducing dependence on imports and foreign assistance. Equity means ensuring that development benefits are more fairly shared.

In agriculture, land reforms sought to change ownership and tenancy arrangements. The Green Revolution combined high-yielding variety seeds with irrigation and fertilisers, raising food-grain output and supporting self-sufficiency, though gains were uneven across crops and regions. In industry, the 1956 Industrial Policy Resolution gave public enterprises a major role; small-scale industries received support, and import substitution used tariffs and restrictions to protect domestic producers.

Keep the distinction clear

Import substitution aimed to produce at home goods that had previously been imported. It supported domestic capacity, while prolonged protection could also reduce competitive pressure.

Answer focus Define the planning goal, then connect it to a policy example such as land reform, public sector industry, or import substitution.

IE 3 · 6 MARKS · 18 PERIODS

Liberalisation, Privatisation and Globalisation

The 1991 reforms followed severe balance-of-payments pressure, a shortage of foreign exchange, and fiscal stress. The reforms changed the role of government controls, private enterprise, and India’s links with the world economy.

Three related, distinct changes

Liberalisation reduced controls and licensing and changed industrial, financial, tax, trade, and investment policies. Privatisation increased the role of private enterprise, including through disinvestment in public enterprises. Globalisation connected the Indian economy more closely to international trade, investment, technology, and services.

Reforms can encourage competition, efficiency, investment, and access to wider markets. Their effects are not identical across sectors and groups. Greater competition can place pressure on less productive firms; gains may be unevenly distributed, and dependence on external markets can create exposure to global changes.

Explain the three terms separately

For a strong long answer, set out the 1991 context, define each reform, give a policy example, then assess both potential gains and concerns.

Answer structure Context → liberalisation → privatisation → globalisation → balanced assessment.

IE 4 · 6 MARKS · 20 PERIODS

Poverty

Poverty is the inability to meet basic needs. Looking only at income can miss overlapping hardships such as poor health, limited education, unsafe living conditions, debt, and restricted opportunities.

From causes to responses

Low productivity and uneven development can limit earnings and access to work. Unemployment and underemployment weaken household income; unequal access to assets, education, health services, and opportunity can make poverty persist across generations. Price rises can also put pressure on households with little room in their budgets.

A poverty line is a threshold used to classify people by income or consumption under a chosen method. It is useful for tracking one dimension, but it cannot by itself show how severe deprivation is or whether people can reach quality education, health care, water, or other services. Multidimensional measures therefore examine several deprivations together.

Broad responses include employment and productivity growth, targeted poverty programmes, and reliable access to essential services. Growth matters, but its effects depend on who can participate and whether basic services reach people facing deprivation.

Do not quote old poverty thresholds as current

The notes include committee thresholds and dated estimates. Treat those as historical methods or examples only; use a current textbook or teacher-approved source for present-day figures.

Answer focus Define poverty, explain several connected causes, then describe and assess responses. Keep dated monetary thresholds out of present-day claims.

IE 5 · 4 MARKS · 11 PERIODS

Human Capital Formation in India

Human capital is the knowledge, skills, experience, and health embodied in people. Investment in people can improve their capabilities and support productivity, while human development also values well-being as an end in itself.

How capabilities are built

The main sources are education, health, on-the-job training, migration, and access to information. Education develops knowledge and skills; health supports the ability to learn and work; training adds job-specific skills. Migration may help workers access better opportunities, and information helps people make decisions about education, employment, and the use of skills.

Human capital differs from physical capital: a machine or factory is a produced input, while human capital is embodied in a person. Public and private provision both contribute to education and health, but unequal access and inefficient delivery can limit the results of investment.

Human capital and human development are related, not identical

Human capital emphasizes capabilities as a means to productivity. Human development is broader and treats health, education, and well-being as valuable in their own right.

Answer focus Name the source of investment, explain what capability it builds, and connect it to productivity or well-being.

IE 6 · 5 MARKS · 13 PERIODS

Rural Development

Rural development improves livelihoods and living conditions in rural areas. It includes agriculture, but also depends on credit, markets, infrastructure, and work beyond crop cultivation.

Four connected levers

Rural credit: farmers and small enterprises need timely, affordable institutional loans for inputs, equipment, and working capital. Agricultural marketing: weak storage, transport, market information, and bargaining power can reduce the price farmers receive; regulated markets, cooperatives, and infrastructure can address parts of this problem.

Diversification: allied activities such as dairy, poultry, fisheries, and non-farm work can add income and reduce dependence on one crop or season. Organic farming: reducing reliance on synthetic inputs can support soil and ecological health, but transition costs, yields during transition, certification, and market access can pose challenges.

Connect the pieces

Credit helps producers invest; infrastructure connects them to markets; diversification spreads income risk. A programme in one area works better when the other links are available.

Answer focus Organise a five-mark response around credit, marketing, diversification, and organic farming. Explain how each supports livelihoods.

IE 7 · 3 MARKS · 8 PERIODS

Employment: Growth, Informalisation and Other Issues

Employment is about both the number of people working and the quality and security of their work. Economic growth matters when it creates productive opportunities and improves working conditions.

Classify work accurately

Workers may be self-employed, regular salaried, or casual wage workers. Casual workers are hired for short periods and generally have less security; regular workers receive more stable employment and may have social-security benefits. The formal/informal distinction concerns the conditions, protections, and organization of work.

The labour force includes people who are working and people seeking and available for work. The workforce counts people actually employed. Disguised unemployment occurs when more workers are engaged in a task than are needed, so some workers can leave without reducing output. Seasonal unemployment occurs when work is available only during parts of the year.

Labour force is not the same as workforce

People seeking work belong to the labour force but are not part of the employed workforce. Keep this distinction clear when explaining unemployment.

Answer focus For a short answer, define the terms precisely and use a brief example of seasonal or disguised unemployment.

IE 8 · 6 MARKS · 17 PERIODS

Environment and Sustainable Development

The environment supports economic activity and life. It supplies resources, absorbs some waste, sustains ecological systems, and provides aesthetic value. These functions have limits.

Development within ecological limits

Carrying capacity means resource use should remain within the environment’s ability to regenerate resources. Absorptive capacity means waste should stay within the environment’s ability to absorb and process it. If extraction exceeds regeneration or waste exceeds absorption, environmental quality and future production are put at risk.

Sustainable development meets present needs while preserving the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Strategies include renewable energy such as solar and wind, cleaner fuels and biogas, organic farming, and practices that conserve resources. The appropriate choice depends on local conditions and its full environmental and social effects.

Two capacities, two limits

Carrying capacity concerns resource replenishment. Absorptive capacity concerns waste. State both clearly, then connect a policy to the limit it helps address.

Answer structure Explain the environment’s functions, define both capacities, define sustainable development, and connect strategies to the pressures they reduce.

IE 9 · 3 MARKS · 11 PERIODS

Comparative Development: India and its Neighbours

Comparing India, China, and Pakistan helps show how policy choices, timing, and institutions shape development. A fair comparison uses the same indicators for each country and explains what the evidence suggests.

Compare strategies, then interpret

China experienced the Great Leap Forward, the Cultural Revolution, and reforms beginning in 1978. For Pakistan, it points to a mixed-economy approach, import substitution, and the Green Revolution. For India, consider planning and the policy path discussed in the preceding chapters.

Compare dimensions such as growth patterns, sectoral change, population and social indicators, and policy sequence. Country statistics change over time, and definitions may differ. Check the current class text before quoting numerical comparisons.

Use like-for-like indicators

Put the same measure beside each country, use the same time period and definition, and finish with an interpretation. A list of unrelated figures does not explain comparative development.

Answer focus Choose two or three comparable indicators, identify a policy difference, and state what the comparison helps explain.
